# The Freelancer's AI Proposal Pack

The exact proposal system I use with freelance clients. Three templates, one extraction prompt, a follow-up sequence and the onboarding kit. Clone it, fill in the variables, ship.

The honest framing first. AI doesn't write your proposal. You write it once, well, in your voice. Then AI takes the brief from each new prospect and adapts the proposal to fit. The output is 80% there in ten minutes. The remaining 20% is the human read-through that makes it actually sound like you.

Done right, your proposal-writing time drops from two hours per opportunity to roughly fifteen minutes. The proposals also get more consistent, because the underlying structure is locked in.

## Template 1: the project proposal

For one-off projects with a clear scope. Most defensible structure I've found.

### Structure

1. **The situation as I understand it** (one paragraph, in their language)
2. **What success looks like for you** (specific metrics, deadlines, deliverables)
3. **What I'd do about it** (the approach, not the tasks)
4. **What's in scope** (clear, bulleted)
5. **What's out of scope** (the protection against scope creep)
6. **The timeline** (week-by-week or phase-by-phase)
7. **Investment** (the price, presented confidently, not buried)
8. **What I need from you** (the dependencies)
9. **Next step** (one specific action — sign, call, reply)

Length target: 800 to 1,200 words. Long enough to feel considered. Short enough that someone busy will read it.

### Why this works

Most freelance proposals lead with the freelancer ("about me, my services, my process"). The buyer doesn't care yet. They want to know you understand their situation. The first two sections do that work. By the time you get to your approach, they're already nodding.

## Template 2: the retainer proposal

For ongoing relationships. Different shape because the question isn't "can you do this thing" but "can I trust you with this thing every month."

### Structure

1. **Where you are now** (current state, in their words)
2. **Where you want to be in 6 months** (their stated goal, plus one you'd add if I were them)
3. **What ongoing support looks like** (cadence, comms, what gets shipped each month)
4. **The retainer scope** (what's included, what's out, what's bolt-on)
5. **The first 30 days** (the disproportionately important onboarding period)
6. **Monthly investment** (the price, with what changes if scope changes)
7. **How we know it's working** (the success metric, reviewed quarterly)
8. **How we end if it's not** (the off-ramp — included on purpose)
9. **Next step**

Length: 1,000 to 1,400 words. The off-ramp section matters more than people think — it makes saying yes safer.

## Template 3: the paid-discovery proposal

For prospects with a vague brief, ambitious goals, and no clear scope. Charging for discovery is what separates freelancers who win these from freelancers who burn weeks on free scoping.

### Structure

1. **What we don't know yet** (the open questions a real plan can't answer)
2. **Why a paid discovery is the honest answer** (not a hedge, a stance)
3. **What the discovery produces** (deliverables: written plan, scope, fixed price for phase two)
4. **Timeline** (1 to 2 weeks, usually)
5. **Investment** (£X for the discovery, credited against phase two if they proceed)
6. **What happens if you don't proceed** (they keep the plan, no obligation)
7. **Next step**

Length: 500 to 800 words. The shorter, the more confident it reads.

## The brief-to-proposal prompt

The single biggest time-saver in the pack. Paste this with your filled-in template and the client's brief.

```
You are a senior freelance [discipline] writing a proposal in your own voice.
Below you have:

1. Your standard [project / retainer / paid-discovery] proposal template,
   already filled out for a similar past client.
2. The new client's brief.

Your job is to adapt the template to the new brief. Do not invent details
you can't see in the brief. Where the brief is silent on a section, flag
it for the human author to fill in (use square brackets, e.g.
[CLARIFY WITH CLIENT: their current marketing budget]).

The proposal must:
- Open with the situation as the client described it, in their language
- Use the same structure and tone as the template
- Quote actual numbers, deadlines and dependencies from the brief —
  never paraphrase to be safe
- Be 800 to 1,200 words (project / paid-discovery) or 1,000 to 1,400
  (retainer)
- Sign off with the same specific next step as the template

Voice rules: no buzzwords ("leverage", "synergy"), no consultant-vague,
short sentences carry weight. UK English. Direct.

Your standard template (already filled out for a similar past client):
[paste filled-in template here]

New client's brief:
[paste brief here]
```

Run this once per new opportunity. Read the output. Edit the bracketed gaps and the bits that don't sound quite like you. Ship.

## The follow-up sequence

What to send if the proposal goes quiet. Four emails over fourteen days.

### Email 1: day 3 — the short check-in

Subject: Quick check on the [project name] proposal

```
Hi [Name],

Just checking the proposal I sent on [date] landed and made sense. Happy to
jump on a 15-minute call this week if it'd be useful to walk through any
of it, or to answer questions over email if that's easier.

Either way, no pressure on timing.

Chris
```

### Email 2: day 7 — the value add

Subject: One thing I'd add to the proposal

```
Hi [Name],

Was thinking about [their problem] over the weekend and wanted to add one
thing to the proposal that didn't make it in the first draft.

[Specific, useful observation about their situation — not a sales angle]

Happy to update the proposal if any of this changes how you're thinking
about it.

Chris
```

### Email 3: day 11 — the gentle disqualifier

Subject: Should I close this one out?

```
Hi [Name],

Realised I haven't heard back on [project name]. Totally fine if this isn't
the right time or if you've gone in a different direction — wanted to ask
directly rather than assume.

Should I close it out, or is it just been a busy fortnight?

Chris
```

### Email 4: day 14 — the close-the-loop

Subject: Closing the loop on [project name]

```
Hi [Name],

Closing this one out on my side so I can free up the slot. If the timing
shifts in a few weeks or months, just reply to this thread — I keep
proposals warm for 90 days.

Best,
Chris
```

The day-11 email converts more replies than the other three combined. Most people don't reply because they're embarrassed about not deciding, not because they're not interested. Giving them an easy way to say "still thinking" or "go away" gets a real answer fast.

## The onboarding kit

Three emails sent in the 48 hours after a "yes."

**Email 1: the welcome (within 1 hour of yes).** Thank them, confirm the next step, set expectations for what's happening this week.

**Email 2: the kickoff agenda (24 hours later).** What we'll cover in the kickoff call, what they need to bring, what they should have ready.

**Email 3: the week-one check-in (5 days in).** Quick "here's where we are, here's what's next, anything I should know?" Demonstrates rhythm and momentum without needing a meeting.

These three emails do more for the relationship than the first month of project work. New clients are anxious — they just spent money. The job in week one is to make them feel like spending it was the right call.

## LinkedIn extraction prompt

Take a finished project and turn it into 3 to 5 LinkedIn posts. Run this once per project wrap.

```
You are helping a senior freelance [discipline] write LinkedIn posts about
a recently completed project.

The post must:
- Be 100 to 200 words
- Open with a hook in the first line (visible above "see more")
- Tell one specific story from the project — what was the problem, what
  did we try, what worked
- Avoid any client identifier the client hasn't agreed to share
- End with either a concrete takeaway the reader can use, or one specific
  question
- Sound like the freelancer wrote it, not a marketing agency

Generate 5 posts from the project below, each from a different angle:
1. The specific problem
2. The unexpected technical detail
3. The decision point that changed the project
4. The result, with the number
5. The lesson for anyone considering similar work

Project details:
[paste project notes / final write-up]
```

Run this on the last Friday of every project. Schedule the posts across the next month. Inbound enquiries spike when there's a steady cadence of real work being talked about.

## When proposals don't convert

If your win rate drops below 30%, the issue is almost always one of these:

- **The brief was vague and you didn't charge for discovery.** You wrote a free 3-hour proposal and got beaten on price by someone who did the same. Charge for discovery.
- **You led with yourself.** Section 1 was "about me" instead of "the situation as I understand it." Buyers tune out by paragraph two.
- **The price was buried.** Find your price. Confidently put it where the buyer expects it. Don't make them hunt for it.
- **The next step was vague.** "Let me know if you'd like to chat" loses. "Reply yes to start Monday" wins. Pick one specific action.
